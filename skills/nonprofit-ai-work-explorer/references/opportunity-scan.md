# Explore a range of opportunities

Use this when the participant wants ideas across their work. The goal is a small,
varied set of possibilities they recognize and can judge. They may keep the
shortlist without selecting anything to implement.

## Discover enough breadth

Start with the work they describe, then follow a few different threads. A recent
working week, a departmental responsibility or a mission activity can provide
concrete examples. Do not demand that the first example be painful or repetitive.

Think about areas such as learning and research; writing and communication;
preparing or rehearsing conversations; checking and making sense of information;
accessibility and translation; coordination and recurring administration; and
program or service support. These are private prompts for noticing gaps, not a
list of departments, mandatory questions, or assumed duties.

Ask one question at a time. Find out about work that already goes well, something
they want more capacity for, or something they would like to become able to do.
Do not spend the entire scan diagnosing the first task. If they name several
areas at once, use those rather than asking them to repeat each one.

Enough breadth usually means you can explain several genuinely different
activities and their desired outcomes. If the participant's role is narrow or
they have little time, fewer opportunities are fine. A third phrasing of the same
newsletter is not a third area of work.

When they cannot picture a capability, offer a brief fictional example tied to
something they said. If they request the example, that request is sufficient to
show it. Use the example to inform their judgment, then continue the scan unless
they choose to focus. Do not assume their interest proves usefulness.

## From their account to a shortlist

Summarize the different activities, their goals and the boundaries that would
change the ideas. Ask for correction and wait, unless they explicitly request
early provisional ideas. Preserve reported concerns and uncertainty.

Normally suggest three to five possibilities supported by their account. Use
fewer if fewer earn a place. Do not manufacture needs or recommend consequential
decisions about people. Include an opportunity beyond drafting when their work
supports one; do not force artificial variety.

For each possibility, say briefly:

- The piece of work it relates to and why it fits what they described.
- The possible AI contribution and benefit, clearly stated as a proposal.
- The main limitation or decision to establish before using it.
- A simpler existing or non-AI route when that is a credible alternative.

Two to four short sentences per item are usually enough. A table may help if
the items are easy to compare. Avoid long implementation plans or a catalogue
of named products at this stage. Use the task level if a product's current
capability or data settings have not been verified.

## Keep exploration separate from implementation

Permission to explore an idea is different from permission to use organizational
material in a tool or change other people's work. Known constraints still shape
the shortlist. Unknown tool or data approval can be written as an open question;
it does not prevent imagining a public or fictional example.

Do not ask for several approval decisions per speculative idea. Do not promise
that an idea is permitted, safe, accurate, affordable or useful until the
relevant facts support that claim. Do not nominate a colleague for a trial from
one person's account of their interest.

If the person wants only the shortlist, finish there. Otherwise offer a choice
to keep it for later or investigate one idea. When they choose an idea, carry
what is known into the focused interview; ask only the missing questions that
affect its fit or a trial. A process improvement or a reason to wait remains a
valid result.

## A short take-away

State the scope, why these opportunities fit, and the shortlist with important
unknowns. Keep facts and proposals distinct. There is no obligation to add an
owner, budget, tool choice, experiment, reminder or deadline to every idea.
Close without pushing the participant to launch something.
